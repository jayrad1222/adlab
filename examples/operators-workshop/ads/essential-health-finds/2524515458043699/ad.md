Six Winters, Somebody Plowed Her Driveway Before Dawn and Never Said a Word. When She Finally Caught Him, He Explained It in Nine.

Picture a gravel road in a county where the snow means it.

Picture a woman at the end of it, at five in the morning, standing at her window with a coffee, looking at a driveway that has already been cleared.

Call her Corinne.

And picture the truck two properties down with its lights still on and a blade on the front, pulling back onto the road. Call the man in it Herb.

This has happened every storm for six years. Corinne has never asked him to. She has never successfully thanked him.

And the morning she finally catches him, he's going to explain the whole thing in nine words, and those nine words are going to reorganize her kitchen.

Let me back up. You need Corinne first, and you have met her.

Corinne is fifty-eight, and for nineteen years she has driven a school bus.

Which means her day is not a day. It's three pieces of a day with holes cut between them.

She's on the road at five-forty. Route by six-fifteen. Done by eight-thirty, home by nine.

Then the hole: the middle of the day, which sounds like freedom and functions like a waiting room, because the afternoon route starts at two-fifteen and everything in her life has to fit around the fact that a bus is going to need her at two-fifteen.

Home again by four-thirty. Some nights, an activity run to a ball game two towns over, back at ten.

Corinne does everything right.

Look at what she owns.

There's a stationary bike in the spare room, bought after real research, the good one that people say lasts.

There's a sewing machine — her mother's, rebuilt at a shop two counties over, because Corinne wanted it done properly.

That's what makes the next part strange, and it's the thing Corinne has never once been able to explain to herself.

None of it runs.

Not from lack of wanting. Not from lack of trying — she starts things constantly, and she starts them well, which is somehow worse.

She'd tell you, if you asked her at a church supper and she'd had a glass of wine, that she does everything right and it doesn't take. That she has the equipment of a person with a good life and the calendar of a person who's waiting for one.
