## 2. Sophistication stage and the required move

**Stage 3 to 4.** Mechanism demos from 5 of 9 advertisers (the slide, the pockets opening, the compression pump, wear over time); identification from 6 of 9 (made by the girls; still carry cash; football fans; durability buyers); bigger claims from 4 (best wallet ever; #1 bestselling; 50,000 reviews). Stage 5 is visible at Bellroy (the ad is the experience of owning one for a thousand days).

**Required move:** identification carried by a proof competitors cannot fake. For a metal wallet that is wear over years (Bellroy does it for leather; nobody does it for metal), the switch ritual (what you take out of the old wallet), and the tactile mechanism (Dango's sound-on demo, once). Not another superlative and not a deeper discount.

## 4. Uncontested claims (true of a metal wallet brand, said by nobody in the sample)

- **The metal after five years.** Bellroy shows leather at day 1,000; no advertiser shows metal plates after years in a pocket. Observed gap; the member would need real, dated wallets to show it.
- **The switch.** Nobody shows the moment of moving cards out of a bifold and leaving the rest behind. The ritual is the ad.
- **What falls out, and what does not.** The gadget fear (cards sliding out, the clip scratching) is never addressed; the 99-day trial exists on the site precisely for it, and no ad dramatizes "live with it for 99 days".
- **The back pocket.** The fat-wallet posture and the suit-pocket bulge are absent from every ad; the category sells the object, not the problem it ends.

## 6. Proof arms race

Roster proof types (advertisers of 9): none 8, demo 6, UGC 3, social-proof 2, authority 1. The race is unboxings and unsourced counts. Bellroy's worn wallet is the only proof object that cannot be bought; Ekster rents authority. A dated, real wallet with years of wear, shown by the person who carried it, would be distinctive and checkable.

The map says the room above "always on sale" is empty except for Bellroy. A price stated once with its reason, and the 99-day trial told as a story, would be a different signal from everything in the sample.

## 8. Format mix and gaps

Roster formats (advertisers of 9): demo video 6, carousel catalogue 5, testimonial or UGC 4, offer card 3, comparison 2, founder-to-camera 2, how-it's-made or unboxing 1, listicle 2, before-and-after 1 (Bellroy). ## 11. Ten testable hypotheses

1. A problem-first static (the bulge in a suit pocket, the cards on the car floor) selects the switch-moment persona better than any product tile. Test: hook rate versus a catalogue tile.
2. A real, dated wallet after years in a pocket, shown by its owner, beats an unboxing on hold rate. Test: same body, two openers.
3. "Live with it for 99 days" told as a story (what week one, week five and week twelve feel like) lifts CTR over the bare trial line. Test: CTR.
4. Naming what you give up (coins, receipts) and what you keep (cash in the clip) raises CTR among the right buyer and lowers it among the wrong one. Test: CTR and return rate.
5. The sound-on slide demo (Dango's single ad) works for Ridge's mechanism with a plain "listen" caption. Test: hook rate.
6. A gift-buyer callout ("for the man who says he does not need anything") outperforms team-logo identity for the holiday window. Test: hook rate and conversion.
7. Two pockets, two wallets (Ekster's comparison format applied to wallets) makes slimness legible without the word "slim". Test: hold rate.
8. Stating the price once, with the reason, beats a discount banner on contribution per order even if CTR drops. Test: economics, not CTR.
9. A comment-as-hook ad built on a real customer comment (consent recorded) beats a creator unboxing on hook rate. Test: hook rate.
10. A machining or assembly clip (how it's made, which nobody runs) carries the engineered identity better than finish drops. Test: hold rate.
