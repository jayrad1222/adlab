**Title:** Feel Like Yourself Again

**Primary text:**

The first time I couldn't get up the ladder, I told my apprentice my back went out.

It hadn't.

I've torn off probably 800 roofs in my life and I know what a back going out feels like. It feels like someone shoved a knife between your spine and your kidney and twisted. This wasn't that. This was something else.

This was my knees forgetting they were knees.

My name is Greg. I'm 53. I'm a roofer in Fort Wayne, Indiana, and I've been on roofs since I was 17 years old. My dad was a roofer. His dad framed houses but did roofs in the summer to make extra money. I started Helton Roofing with my brother Dale in 1998 and we've been the guys you call in Allen County when a tree comes down on your garage or the shingles you put on in the 90s finally give up.

I'm telling you this because I want you to understand something before I tell you the rest of the story.

Roofing is not a job. It's a body. You don't roof for 36 years and have a body left over for other things. You spend your whole life giving pieces of yourself to other people's houses. Your knees go to the pitch. Your back goes to the bundles. Your shoulders go to the nail gun. Your hands go to the heat and the cold and the splinters and the cuts.

You know going in that you're trading.

You just don't know what the bill's going to look like when it comes due.

Mine came due on a Tuesday morning in May of last year, on a tear off in Aboite Township, on a ranch house with a 6/12 pitch and three layers of shingles that had to come off before lunch.

My apprentice that day was a kid named Tyler. 22 years old. Been with us about a year. Good kid, dumb in the way 22 year olds are dumb, hard worker, the kind of guy who'd jump off the truck and start tearing into something before I'd even finished telling him what we were doing.

We pulled up at 7:15. Tyler dropped the trailer. I went to set the ladder.

I want you to picture the ladder. 24 footer. Aluminum. The kind I've climbed maybe 40,000 times in my life. I could climb that ladder in my sleep. I have climbed it half asleep, hungover, with a fever, with a cut on my hand bleeding into my work glove, with my back so locked up I couldn't sit down.

I started up.

Rung one. Rung two. Rung three.

Rung four, my right knee made a noise I'd never heard come out of my body before.

It wasn't a pop. It wasn't a crack. It was kind of a wet sound. Like something inside the joint shifting around looking for a place to land.

And then my knee just stopped working.

Not painfully, exactly. It was worse than that. It just stopped responding. Like the wires between my brain and my leg got cut. I told my knee to bend. My knee did not bend. I told it again. Nothing.

I was four rungs up a ladder with my left foot on rung four and my right foot trying to get to rung five and not getting there.

I looked down. Tyler was at the bottom holding the ladder, staring up at me with a confused look on his face.

I said, "Come up here a second."

He climbed up behind me. I told him my back went out. I said I needed help getting down. He believed me because why wouldn't he. We got me down. I sat on the tailgate of the truck for 20 minutes. Tyler ran to the gas station and got me a Gatorade. I told him to call Dale and have him send another guy.

I drove home that afternoon with the worst feeling I have ever had in my life sitting in the seat next to me.

It wasn't pain.

It was a question.

The question was: Am I done?

I'm going to back up and tell you about the year before, because the ladder wasn't really the moment things broke. The ladder was the moment I had to admit they'd already broken.

Things had been going wrong for about 18 months.

It started in my hands. I'd be holding a nail gun and my fingers would just kind of seize up around the trigger. Not painfully. Just stuck. I'd have to put the gun down and shake my hand out for a minute and then go back to it. I figured it was the cold. We were in the middle of a January and I was pulling 10 hour days in 22 degree weather.

Then it was my shoulders. I'd be carrying a bundle of shingles up a ladder, 80 pounds on my shoulder, and halfway up I'd feel something in my rotator like a wet rope being pulled through a pipe. Not a tear. Something duller. I'd get to the top and have to set the bundle down and roll my shoulder for a minute before I could move.

Then it was my hips. I'd be sitting on the ridge of a roof drinking coffee on a break and when I went to stand up, my hips wouldn't unfold. Like they'd locked into the position I was sitting in. I'd have to grab the ridge with both hands and kind of pull myself up like an old man getting out of a recliner.

I was 52 years old.

52 is not old. My dad was working roofs at 67. My uncle Bert was on a ladder at 71. The guy who taught me how to swing a hammer, a man named Ralph who passed in 2011, was nailing down shingles at 74 and only stopped because his wife made him.

52 is not old in the roofing world.

But my body was acting like 70.

I went to my doctor. Dr. Reisman. He's been my doctor since the kids were in elementary school. Good guy. Late 50s. Plays golf. The kind of doctor who listens for about four minutes and then writes something down and tells you to lose weight.

I told him about my hands. My shoulders. My hips. My knees, which had started doing the thing where they'd ache for the first 20 minutes of every morning and then loosen up after I got moving.

He nodded. He moved my arms around. He pressed on my knees. He had me bend forward and touch my toes. I got down to my shins.

He sat back on his stool and said, "Greg, I'm going to be straight with you. You're 52 years old, you've been roofing since high school, and you're paying the bill. There's nothing structurally wrong here. This is wear and tear. This is what 36 years of roofs looks like."

I said, "What do I do about it?"

He said, "You can take ibuprofen for the pain. You can do some stretching. You can think about getting off the roof and into the office side of the business."

He said it like it was nothing. Like he was telling me to switch from a hardback to a softer chair.

Get off the roof.

I drove home that day in a kind of fog I'd never been in before. Get off the roof. The roof was where I lived. The roof was the only thing I'd ever been good at. My brother handles the books, the bidding, the customer calls. I do the work. I climb. I tear off. I lay down felt. I shingle. I'm the guy on the ladder.

Get off the roof was the same as saying get out of your own life.

I didn't tell my wife Patty. I didn't tell Dale. I just started taking the ibuprofen. Three in the morning. Three at lunch. Three before bed. Sometimes a fourth set when I got home if my knees were really yelling.

For about a month it helped. Took the edge off enough that I could keep moving.

Then it stopped helping. The pain came back through the medicine like water through a screen door.

So I doubled the dose. Six in the morning. Six at lunch. Six at night.

About six weeks into that, my stomach started bleeding. I didn't know that's what it was at first. I thought I had a stomach bug. Then the toilet bowl was black one morning and Patty saw it before I could flush.

We went to the ER. They told me I had two ulcers in my stomach. They told me to stop the ibuprofen immediately or I'd be on the operating table inside a year.

I stopped the ibuprofen.

The pain came roaring back inside 48 hours. Worse than before, somehow, like it had been waiting in the closet the whole time and now it was loose in the house.

That's when the ladder thing happened.

I'm sitting on the tailgate of my truck in Aboite Township that Tuesday morning, knee not working, Gatorade in my hand, and I'm doing math in my head.

The mortgage. The truck payment. Dale's kid is going to Purdue in the fall. Patty's been talking about retiring in five years. We've got a granddaughter, our daughter Megan's little girl Hailey, she's 4 years old and she calls me Pop and I am her favorite person on this earth.

How am I supposed to be Pop if I can't pick her up?

How am I supposed to take her fishing at Cedarville Reservoir like I told her I would, when I can't kneel down to bait the hook?

How am I supposed to walk her down the aisle if she ever gets that far, when I can barely walk to the mailbox without my knees burning?

I sat on that tailgate for 20 minutes and I felt every year of every roof come down on top of me at once.

Then I drove home.

I told Patty that night. The whole thing. The hands, the shoulders, the hips, the ladder, Reisman telling me to get off the roof. The ulcers. The math.

She didn't say anything for a long time. She got up and made me a sandwich and put it in front of me. Then she sat down across from me at the kitchen table and she said something that's stuck with me.

She said, "Greg, you need to stop trying to bandage this. You need to figure out what's actually going on inside you."

I didn't know what she meant. I don't think she knew what she meant either. But the sentence stuck.

Two weeks later, our neighbor Kenny came by to borrow the pressure washer.

After the plant closed he started a small landscaping operation, mowing yards and doing fall cleanups for the neighborhood. He has hands like leather and a permanent farmer's tan and a Chevy with 230,000 miles on it.

He said, "You look terrible."

I said, "Thanks, Kenny."

He sat down on the step. He said, "What's going on with you?"

So I told him. Most of it. Not the part about Hailey. The rest of it.

Kenny listened. He pulled a piece of dried grass out of his pant cuff and rolled it between his fingers while he listened.

When I was done he said, "Greg. You remember the year I couldn't get out of my truck?"

I'd forgotten about it because Kenny had been fine for years now. Mowing his yards. Climbing in and out of the truck like a 30 year old.

He looked at me and said, "My wife's sister sent me something. From India, originally. Some kind of berry."

The thing they don't tell you about joint pain is that the joint isn't the whole story.

The joint is where you feel the pain. But what's causing the pain is mostly happening at a level you can't see, in the cells that surround the joint and the tissues that connect the joint to everything else.

The second is oxidative stress. The fancy way of saying your cells are getting rusted. Free radicals are produced naturally by your body, but as you age, especially if you've been hard on your body for decades, the rate of damage outruns the rate of repair. Cartilage cells take the hit. Tendon cells take the hit. The synovial fluid that's supposed to lubricate the joint thins out. The whole system starts running rough.
