# Voss toolkit (Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, *Never Split the Difference*, 2016)

## The idea in three sentences

Voss, a former FBI hostage negotiator, argues that negotiations are won by making the other side feel understood, not by argument. His tools all serve that end: tactical empathy, labeling, the accusation audit, mirroring, calibrated questions, no-oriented questions, and the "that's right" moment. In ads, labels and accusation audits are the best objection-handling hooks in this library, because they say the viewer's doubt before the viewer does.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- Labels: sentences that open "It seems like," "It sounds like," "It looks like." Example: "It seems like you've paid for one of these before and it sat in a tab."
- Accusation audit: the ad lists its own objections first. "You're thinking this is another course. You're thinking the testimonials are paid. You're thinking you don't have the time."
- Mirroring: the ad repeats the last few words of a real comment or review and builds from them.
- Calibrated questions: open "how" or "what" questions that have no yes-or-no answer. "How are you supposed to fill a group nobody has heard of?"
- No-oriented questions: "Would it be ridiculous to," "Is it a bad idea to," "Have you given up on."
- The "that's right" moment: a summary of the viewer's situation so exact that the comments say "this is me." Count those comments; they are the evidence the tool worked.
- Silence after a label. In video, a beat of pause after the label line, with no explanation.

## Use it in ours

Each tool, the copy move it becomes, and one original hook.

- Tactical empathy. Write the viewer's situation from their side before saying anything about ours. Hook: "You didn't start a community so you could spend Sunday night writing posts nobody answers."
- Labeling. State the viewer's feeling or suspicion as an observation, then stop. Hook: "It seems like you've already been burned by a growth program once." Rule: label, then silence. Do not explain the label.
- Accusation audit. List the worst things the viewer could think about the ad, in order, then answer the one that matters. Hook: "You're thinking this is another course from a guy with a rented car. Fair. Here's the one thing that's different."
- Mirroring. Open with the last three to five words of a real review or comment, in quotation marks, then continue. Hook: "'Still stuck at twelve members.' That line keeps showing up in the comments. Here is why."
- Calibrated questions. Ask a how or what question the viewer cannot answer with no, so they answer it in their head. Hook: "How are you supposed to fill a group when the people who need it have never heard of you?"
- No-oriented questions. Ask a question whose honest answer is no, which the viewer finds easier to give than yes. Hook: "Would it be a bad idea to let one post a week do the selling for you?"
- The "that's right" moment. End the body with a summary of the viewer's situation in their own terms. The target is a comment that says "that's exactly it." Body close: "So you have a good product, a small group, and no idea how strangers are supposed to find it. That's the whole problem."

By format:

- Hook: label or accusation audit. These two select the skeptic, who is the viewer most other ads lose.
- Body: mirror a real comment, then a calibrated question, then the mechanism.
- Static: one label as the headline. Nothing under it except the CTA.
- Video: label at the open, a beat of silence, then the audit, then the mechanism. The silence is the edit, not an accident.
- Landing page: an accusation audit as the second section, each objection with its answer and proof.
- DM or sales page: label first, calibrated question second. Never a yes-oriented question like "do you want to grow?"

## How it backfires

- A label about a personal attribute in a regulated category ("it seems like you're anxious") trips Meta's personal attributes rule. Label the situation, not the person.
- An accusation audit that names a competitor is a comparative claim and a legal risk.
- Fake empathy reads as fake. The label must come from intake, reviews or comments, not from the writer's guess.
- "You're right" is not "that's right." Voss's distinction: the first ends the conversation, the second is agreement. A summary that is close but wrong gets the first.
- Over-use. Three labels in one hook is a tic.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- What is the viewer's strongest suspicion about this ad, and does the ad say it first?
- What would the label be, in one sentence that starts "it seems like"?
- Which real comment or review line could the ad mirror?
- What is the calibrated question the viewer cannot answer with no?
- What summary of their situation would get a "that's right" in the comments?
