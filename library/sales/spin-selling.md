# SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham, *SPIN Selling*, 1988)

## The idea in three sentences

Rackham's team studied recorded sales calls and found that the sellers who closed larger deals asked questions in a sequence: Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff. The implication questions do the heavy lifting, because they make the buyer feel the cost of the problem before any solution is offered. An ad compresses the sequence into seconds: the situation in the callout, the problem in the hook, the implication in one line, the need-payoff as the promise.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- A callout that names a situation, not a product: "Running a two-person agency?" or "Three months into your first paid community?"
- A hook that states a problem in the viewer's own words: "You post every day and the group stays quiet."
- A single implication line that turns the problem into a cost: "Every quiet week is another member you will have to replace."
- A need-payoff phrased as what the viewer gains, not what the product does: "What if the first post each week did the selling for you?"
- A transcript that walks the four beats in order. On a frame readout: situation in the first two seconds, problem by six, implication by ten, payoff after that.
- A static that collapses it to three beats: headline is the problem, sub-head is the implication, button text is the payoff.
- A landing page that opens with "Does this sound like you?" and a bulleted list of problems. That is the Situation and Problem steps rendered as copy.

## Use it in ours

- Hook: lead with the problem in the persona's exact words from intake, reviews or comments. No product name yet.
- Body: one implication line, stated fairly. What the problem costs in time, money or energy. Use the member's own inputs or plain words like "most weeks." Never an invented number.
- Static: headline carries the problem, sub-head carries the implication, button text carries the need-payoff.
- Video: four beats in order, each on its own caption line. Cut on each beat so the edit mirrors the sequence.
- Landing page: open with the situation and a short problem list the viewer can nod along to, then one implication, then the offer framed as the payoff.
- DM or sales page: ask the four questions in order and let the reader answer in their head. "What have you tried? How long has it been like this? What is it costing you? What would change if it were fixed?"

## How it backfires

- Implication lines drift into fear. If the cost is inflated the ad reads as manipulation and the trust score drops.
- In health, finance and body categories, an implication that names a personal attribute in the second person breaks Meta policy. Keep the implication about the situation, not the person.
- Too many questions in a hook feel like an interrogation. One problem, one implication.
- A need-payoff that promises a result you cannot prove is a results claim. Phrase it as a mechanism or a possibility, never a guarantee.
- In a sophisticated market the problem line has been heard a hundred times. The implication must be sharper than the competitor's, or the ad is a copy.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- What situation must the viewer be in for this ad to be for them?
- What is the problem in their words, not ours?
- What does the problem cost them each week, stated fairly?
- What would they gain, in their own terms, if it were solved?
- Which of the four beats does the competitor ad skip, and does the skip hurt or help?
- Is the implication something the viewer already believes, or something we have to prove?
