# Five stages of awareness (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966)

## The idea in three sentences

Every prospect sits at one of five levels: unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or most aware. The ad must open at the level the reader is already at, because an offer shown to someone who does not know they have a problem is noise, and a problem lecture shown to someone ready to buy is friction. Schwartz adds that an ad cannot create desire, only point existing desire at one product, and that the headline's job is to select the reader and promise the payoff of reading on.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- **Most aware:** the first line is the offer, price or deadline. Example: "40% off the annual plan ends Sunday," product named in frame one.
- **Product-aware:** the opening is a difference claim or proof. Example: "Unlike the big gyms, we cap classes at eight."
- **Solution-aware:** the opening explains how it works. Example: "We use a 12-week block, not random workouts, and here is why."
- **Problem-aware:** the opening names the problem better than the viewer can, with no product in the first three seconds. Example: "You open the laptop at 9pm for invoices and close it at 11 having done four."
- **Unaware:** the opening is a story, an identity or a pattern interrupt. Example: a founder video that starts "I quit a job I was good at" and names the product only in the final third.
- **Mismatch cue:** a problem-naming hook followed straight by price. Tag it; it usually means the advertiser is guessing.
- **Stage drift:** older ads problem-led, newer ads offer-led. The audience moved up, or the advertiser ran out of cold-traffic ideas. Note the dates.

## Use it in ours

- **Hook, the opening move per stage:** most aware, state the offer and price; product-aware, state the difference and show proof; solution-aware, name the mechanism; problem-aware, name the problem in the customer's own words from the persona file; unaware, open with a story or an identity the viewer recognizes as theirs.
- **Body:** move the viewer one stage up, not three. A problem-aware ad earns the mechanism, not the price.
- **Static:** problem-named cards and "I was skeptical" quotes for problem-aware and solution-aware; offer cards and comparison charts for product-aware and most aware. Label every bank entry with its stage.
- **Video:** the first three seconds pick the stage. Founder stories for unaware; whiteboard explainers for solution-aware; testimonials and objection handling for product-aware.
- **Landing page:** match the page to the ad. A problem-aware ad lands on a first screen that restates the problem, not a pricing table.
- **Market map:** record which stages the roster covers. When every competitor is product-aware or most aware, the lower stages are open and usually cheaper to reach.

## How it backfires

- Writing for the unaware on a retargeting budget. Story ads need volume to pay back; a small business often cannot wait.
- Treating the stages as a funnel to push people through. One ad rarely moves a viewer two steps.
- Problem-naming in sensitive categories can read as an implied personal attribute under Meta policy. Name the situation, not the person. See [Meta policy pitfalls](../platform/meta-policy-pitfalls.md).
- "You cannot create desire" is a 1966 rule of thumb, not a finding. Use it as a constraint, not a law.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- Which stage is the member's best customer at when they first see the ad?
- Which stage do all the competitors talk to, and which do they ignore?
- Can the member state the problem in words a customer actually used? Where is the evidence?
- What does the customer already believe about the solution category, and does it help or hurt?
- Which existing desire are we pointing at the product, in one line?
