# Mental and physical availability (Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow*, 2010)

## The idea in three sentences

Sharp's evidence from large brand panels says growth comes from reaching many light and non-buyers, not from deepening loyalty among heavy buyers. That reach pays when the brand has mental availability (it comes to mind in the buying situation) and physical availability (it is easy to find and buy when it does). Distinctive brand assets, such as a face, a colour, a sound or a shape, make the ad attributable, so the memory it builds lands on the right brand.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- **Distinctive asset present:** the same founder face, colour block, type treatment or sound across the roster. Example: every static uses the same yellow border and the founder in the same chair. Count how many ads share it.
- **Stock-photo brand:** no recurring asset, generic imagery. Example: fifteen ads where you could swap in a competitor's logo and nothing would look wrong.
- **Buying situation named:** the ad ties the brand to a moment. Example: "when the quarterly tax letter arrives."
- **Reach versus loyalty language:** addressed to the whole category ("if you run a salon") or only to customers ("members, your new feature is here"). Note the ratio.
- **Buying friction:** the ad says plainly how to get it (link, price, location, delivery time) or does not.
- **Brand named early:** brand or founder in the first two seconds or the first line. Attribution only at the end builds memory for the category, not the brand.

## Use it in ours

- **Hook:** tie the hook to a buying situation the persona actually has, in their words, so the brand is filed under that moment.
- **Body:** keep the brand or founder visible and named throughout; do not save the reveal.
- **Static:** pick two or three distinctive assets (founder face, a colour, a repeated phrase) and use them on every card, including the ugly ones. Log them in the asset catalog.
- **Video:** the founder's face and voice are the cheapest asset a small business owns. Open on it, with the brand name on screen inside three seconds.
- **Landing page:** physical availability is the page. Price, what happens next and how fast must be visible without scrolling.
- **Market map:** list each competitor's distinctive assets, or write "none." A category of stock-photo brands is an opening: a consistent face and colour will be remembered when nothing else is.

## How it backfires

- Sharp's findings come from large, mature consumer categories. Whether they transfer to a local service or a few-hundred-member community is contested; take the direction, not the proportions.
- Reach without a working offer burns money. A short runway still needs the ad to convert; see [Binet and Field](binet-field-long-and-short.md).
- Assets become distinctive through repetition. Changing the colour or the founder's framing every round resets the clock.
- A founder as the asset is a single point of failure if they stop wanting to be on camera. Note the risk; do not avoid the asset.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- Which buying situation should the brand come to mind in, and does any ad name it?
- What are the member's distinctive assets, and would a stranger recognize them across three ads?
- What stands between a viewer who wants it and the purchase, and how many steps is that?
- Are the ads talking to current customers or to the many who have never bought?
- Does the brand name appear before the viewer decides to scroll on?
