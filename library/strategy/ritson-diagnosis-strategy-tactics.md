# Diagnosis, strategy, tactics (Mark Ritson, Marketing Week columns and the Mini MBA in Marketing)

## The idea in three sentences

Ritson's order is diagnosis first (research the market and the customer), then strategy (who to target, what position to hold, what objectives to hit), then tactics (the ads, the page, the price). Most small businesses start at tactics, writing ads before they know who the ad is for or what it is meant to change. Ad Lab's sequence, intake, competitors, decode, personas, interrogation, angles, bank, plan, is Ritson's order applied to one deliverable, and the strategy files exist so the bank is never built before the diagnosis.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- **Tactics without strategy:** every ad talks to a different audience with a different promise and no common thread. Example: one ad for beginners, one for agencies, one for "anyone who wants more time," all in the same month.
- **Clear targeting choice:** the ad names one customer and accepts losing others. Example: "Built for plumbers with two to five vans. If you are a one-van shop, this is not for you yet."
- **Stated position:** the ad repeats one idea the brand wants to own. Example: three ads in the roster all return to "the only one with a human answering the phone."
- **Objective visible:** the CTA and the format tell you what the ad is for: awareness (soft CTA, story), consideration (education, comparison), conversion (offer, deadline).
- **No diagnosis:** claims that could belong to any competitor, stock imagery, a hook copied from a bigger brand in another category.
- **Research residue:** customer language, specific situations, a named objection. These usually mean someone listened before writing.

## Use it in ours

- **Hook:** the hook should be traceable to a persona file and a market-map finding. If it cannot cite either, it is a guess.
- **Body:** one position per concept. The body may use several proofs, but all of them serve the same idea.
- **Static:** persona cards and problem-named cards are the tactic that most directly shows the targeting choice. Make one per persona the strategy chose, none for personas it rejected.
- **Video:** founder-to-camera is where the position gets said in plain words. Script the one sentence the brand wants to own and have the founder say it in every video.
- **Landing page:** the page restates the targeting ("for X") and the position above the fold. If the ad and page name different customers, the diagnosis was skipped somewhere.
- **Market map:** this is the diagnosis artefact. It should answer who the competitors target, what position each holds, and which positions and personas are uncontested, before any angle is proposed.

## How it backfires

- Diagnosis as procrastination. A small business can research for months; Ad Lab bounds it to a competitor roster, a persona set and one interrogation, then moves.
- Strategy as a slide. A targeting choice that is never written into a hook is not a strategy.
- Treating the sequence as one-way. The bank's test results are new diagnosis; the plan has to loop back.
- Ritson's own framing is opinionated and his columns argue against much of modern growth marketing. Where his claims about what "most marketers" do are rhetorical rather than measured, treat them as opinion.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- Who is the member choosing to lose? If the answer is nobody, there is no targeting.
- What is the one idea the member wants a customer to associate with them, in a sentence a customer could repeat?
- What does the member want this round of ads to change: being known, being considered, or being bought?
- Which of the member's current ads were written before anyone talked to a customer?
- What did the decode find that the member did not expect, and what does it change?
