# Positioning components (April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences

Dunford's positioning is built in a fixed order: the competitive alternatives (what the customer would do without you, including nothing and a spreadsheet), the unique attributes you have that they lack, the value those attributes enable, the customers who care most about that value, and finally the market category you choose to be judged in. The order matters because each component is derived from the one before, and most businesses start at the end by picking a category and then defending it. Category choice is a Sutherland lever: the same product judged in a different category has a different reference price, different competitors and a different buyer.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- **Alternative named:** the ad says what it replaces. Example: "Stop running your bookings off a group chat and a notebook." The alternative is the real competitor, and "do nothing" is the most common one.
- **Category declared:** the ad says what kind of thing it is. Example: "a CRM for tradespeople" versus "a receptionist in your pocket." Same product, two categories, two reference prices.
- **Category borrowed:** the ad leans on a bigger category's cues (a software demo for what is really a coaching service). Note the mismatch; it often signals a positioning the founder has not decided.
- **Attribute without value:** features listed with no "so that." Example: "AI-powered scheduling" with no consequence named.
- **Best-fit customer stated:** "built for" plus a specific trait. Example: "for studios with more than one instructor."
- **Price out of line with category:** a price far above or below the category's reference. It either signals a different category or a mistake. Record it in the price map.

## Use it in ours

- **Hook:** the strongest Dunford hook names the alternative, not the product. "Still doing X by hand?" selects the viewer who does.
- **Body:** attribute, then value, then who it is for, in that order. Every attribute gets a "which means."
- **Static:** us-versus-them tables are category arguments. Choose the comparison column deliberately: the alternative the customer actually uses, not the competitor the founder resents.
- **Video:** comparison-with-doing-it-alone and myth-correction videos make the category choice explicit. Say the category name on camera.
- **Landing page:** the first screen states the category and the best-fit customer. A visitor should know in five seconds what kind of thing this is and whether it is for them.
- **Market map:** the price and positioning map records the category each competitor has chosen and its reference price. Look for a category nobody in the roster has claimed where the member's attributes are obviously stronger.

## How it backfires

- Inventing a category. A new category with no reference price has to be explained before it can be sold, and a small business cannot fund the explanation. Prefer an existing category where you win.
- Choosing a flattering category the product does not fit. The buyer will test it against the category's expectations and find the gap.
- Comparison claims against named competitors carry legal and platform risk if they cannot be substantiated. See [Meta policy pitfalls](../platform/meta-policy-pitfalls.md).
- Dunford's method comes from B2B software. The ordering transfers well; the examples do not. Translate before applying to a gym or a membership.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- What category are you letting the customer judge you in, and is there a better one?
- What does the customer do instead today, including nothing, and what does that cost them in their words?
- Which attribute do you have that none of the alternatives can claim, and what does it let the customer do?
- Who cares most about that, and is that who the current ads talk to?
- What is the reference price in your chosen category, and does your price make sense against it?
