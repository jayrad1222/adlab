# Loss aversion and prospect theory (Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, "Prospect Theory," 1979)

## The idea in three sentences
Prospect theory says people judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, not as absolute states, and that losses hurt more than equivalent gains please; Kahneman and Tversky's estimates put the ratio at roughly two to one, though the size and universality of loss aversion are contested in later work. For ads, the reference point is the lever: the same offer is a gain from one reference point and a loss avoided from another. Loss frames carry more weight, and because of that they are the frames platform policy watches most closely.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- "Stop losing" language. Example: "stop losing leads to voicemail," rather than "get more leads."
- A reference point stated. Example: "you are paying $400 a month for software you use twice."
- Risk removal framed as protecting what the viewer has. Example: "keep the clients you already won."
- Money-back and "nothing to lose" framing. Example: "try it for 30 days, keep the templates either way."
- Endowed progress. Example: "you are already halfway there" on a quiz or a plan.
- A missed-opportunity frame. Example: "the client who booked with someone else this morning."
- Insurance-style copy in non-insurance categories. Example: "protect your Sundays."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: pick the reference point deliberately. Name what the persona already has and could lose, or what they are already paying.
- Body: pair a loss frame with a concrete gain so the ad does not read as a threat. "Stop losing X; here is the first week."
- Static: a visual of the thing at risk (the empty calendar, the unanswered quote), not a generic warning sign.
- Video: a customer describing what they almost lost, in their words. Avoid narration that tells the viewer what they are losing.
- Landing page: guarantee and refund terms framed as removing the loss; progress indicators on multi-step forms.

## How it backfires
Loss framing is the fastest route to a Meta rejection: alarmist language, implied financial distress, and "you are losing money right now" aimed at a personal situation all breach policy. It is also a trust tax: a brand that only ever threatens trains its audience to feel bad when its ads appear. The effect itself is contested: Gal and Rucker (2018) argue that loss aversion is smaller and less general than claimed, so treat "losses weigh twice gains" as a working rule, not a law, and test the gain frame against the loss frame rather than assuming. In premium categories, aspiration frames often beat loss frames because the buyer wants to feel ambitious, not protected.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What does your customer already have that your product protects?
- What are they currently paying, in money or time, that they do not think of as a cost?
- When a customer describes the decision afterwards, do they say "I gained X" or "I stopped losing Y"?
