# Hyperbolic discounting (Richard Thaler, 1981; George Ainslie; David Laibson, 1997)

## The idea in three sentences
People discount the future steeply and inconsistently: a reward today is worth much more than the same reward next week, but the same gap between week 52 and week 53 barely registers. Thaler (1981) measured this in people's stated trade-offs and Laibson (1997) formalised it as quasi-hyperbolic discounting. For ads this is why "start today," immediate bonuses and fast first results work, and why benefits that arrive in six months need to be made to feel present.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- An immediate deliverable. Example: "get the template the second you sign up," "first session today."
- Time-to-first-result stated. Example: "your first booking within 48 hours."
- Instant bonuses attached to a slow product. Example: a 12-week programme sold with a "today" checklist.
- Present-tense benefit language. Example: "feel the difference tonight" rather than "transform your year."
- Deferred cost. Example: "nothing to pay for 30 days," "pay after your first client."
- Countdown and now-or-later contrast. Example: "start now, or start in January like last year."
- Future benefits made vivid and near. Example: a calendar with a date circled, a "day 7" screenshot.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name what happens today. If the product's value is slow, name the first small thing that is fast.
- Body: sequence benefits from soonest to latest, and give a date for each. "Day 1, day 7, day 30."
- Static: a "today" artefact: the welcome message, the first download, the booked slot.
- Video: show the first five minutes after purchase. A real-time "I just signed up, here is what I got" format.
- Landing page: immediate access, immediate confirmation, and a deferred-payment option if the finance rules allow it. Put the soonest benefit nearest the button.

## How it backfires
"Results today" claims about slow products are false and, in health, fitness and finance categories, policy violations. Deferred payment and "pay later" framing must disclose the full terms. Immediate bonuses can cheapen the core offer if they are the only concrete thing in the ad. The mechanism also makes cancellation a present-tense decision: a buyer who signed up for the fast bonus and never reached the slow benefit churns, so the first-week ritual matters as much as the "today" hook. And a now-or-never urgency frame tips into false scarcity if the offer is actually always available.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What does a customer get in the first ten minutes after buying?
- What is the first real result, and how long does it honestly take?
- If the main benefit takes months, what small, true thing could arrive today?
