# The Illusion of Choice (Richard Shotton, The Illusion of Choice, 2023)

## The idea in three sentences
*The Illusion of Choice* is Shotton's second collection, covering sixteen and a half further biases in the same applied format, with its centre of gravity in friction and memory: make it easy, make it precise, keep it concrete, design the peak and the end. The list below is the subset the seed is confident appears in the book. Verify this list against the book's table of contents before treating it as complete.

### The biases the seed is confident about
Make it easy (friction is the biggest lever and the most ignored); make it precise (precise numbers are more believable than round ones: 1,214 not 1,200); keep it concrete (concrete words are remembered, abstractions are not); the peak-end rule; the generation effect (the audience remembers what they generate themselves); the red sneakers effect (deliberate nonconformity signals status if the person could clearly conform); the Keats heuristic (rhymes feel truer); extremeness aversion; denominator neglect; the power of free; bad is stronger than good (loss frames carry more weight; use with care under policy); choice overload; and humour as a trust and recall device.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Precise numbers. Example: "1,214 clinics," "an average of 11 minutes," not "over a thousand" or "about ten."
- Concrete nouns over abstract claims. Example: "the invoice you have not sent" rather than "cash-flow optimisation."
- A single clear next step. Example: one button, one action, no menu (make it easy).
- A line the viewer has to complete. Example: a question left hanging, a visual with the answer withheld (generation effect).
- A rhyme or near-rhyme in the headline (Keats heuristic).
- Free shipping or a free element emphasised over a discount of the same value (power of free).
- A loss frame. Example: "stop losing" rather than "start gaining."
- Deliberate scruffiness from a brand that could clearly afford polish (red sneakers).
- A joke that lands in the first two seconds (humour).

## Use it in ours
- Hook: a precise number or a concrete object. "The 11pm invoice" beats "late-night admin."
- Body: one idea per sentence, nouns you can picture. Let the viewer complete the thought once.
- Static: fewer words, one action. Count the clicks between the ad and the result and remove one.
- Video: write the ending first. The last three seconds are remembered; make them the best moment.
- Landing page: remove fields, remove choices, show one plan if the persona is new. Rhyme sparingly in the CTA if the brand can carry it.

## How it backfires
Precision must be true: "1,214" that is really "about 1,000" is a false claim. Loss frames in Meta ads tip into alarmist language quickly, and copy that implies the viewer's personal situation is rejected. Humour that mocks the viewer or a group breaches policy and the brand. "Make it easy" taken too far strips the small effort that the IKEA effect depends on. And the red sneakers effect only works for a brand that could obviously conform; scruffiness from an unknown brand reads as cheap.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- Where in your buying process does the customer most often stop? What is the friction there?
- Which of your numbers are rounded, and what is the exact figure?
- What is the last thing a customer experiences with you, and is it the best thing?
