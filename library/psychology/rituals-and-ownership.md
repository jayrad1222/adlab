# Rituals and ownership (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
Products with rituals are stickier: the way you pour the drink, the unboxing, the Sunday planning session, the weekly check-in. Sutherland's point is that the ritual is part of the product's value, not packaging around it, and that a ritual gives the customer a way to own the experience rather than merely consume it. The interrogation asks what the ritual of using your product is, and whether the ad shows it.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- A repeated sequence shown as the product. Example: "every Monday you open the dashboard, pick three leads, send three messages."
- Unboxing or first-use as the creative. Example: the box opened on camera, the components laid out, the first step performed.
- A named routine. Example: "the 10-minute Friday close," "the morning board."
- Time-anchored copy. Example: "day one," "week one," "your first 30 days," with what happens on each.
- Ownership language. Example: "your dashboard," "your plan," "your coach," rather than "the platform."
- A customer performing the ritual unprompted in UGC. Example: a creator who shows their own setup without being asked to.
- Tools or artefacts that only exist because of the ritual. Example: a printed planner, a wall chart, a physical token.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name the ritual, not the outcome. "The Sunday 20 minutes that runs the whole week."
- Body: walk the viewer through the first week in order. Rituals are easier to imagine than results, and imagining use is a step toward buying.
- Static: the artefact of the ritual (the planner, the filled-in chart, the kit laid out) in a real setting.
- Video: a real-time or sped-up performance of the ritual. The unboxing format exists because it works; adapt it to services as "first login" or "first session."
- Landing page: a "your first 30 days" timeline with days and actions. It lowers anxiety and sets up the habit.

## How it backfires
A ritual the business invents for the ad but does not actually deliver is a broken promise and shows up in reviews. Rituals that look like effort put off low-commitment buyers; for an impulse product, "a 20-minute Sunday routine" is friction, not value. "Your first week" timelines are claims about the service and must be true. The mechanism also reverses when the ritual belongs to a competitor's product and the viewer already performs it: showing the same ritual signals "same as what I have."

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What is the ritual of using your product? What does the first week look like, day by day?
- Which part of using it do customers photograph, mention or show other people?
- What would a customer miss if they stopped, other than the result?
