# Perception as a lever (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
Psychological value is real value, because the customer's experience is the only thing they can actually buy. Sutherland's example is the countdown display at a bus stop: it does not make the bus come sooner, it removes the uncertainty, which was the real cost of waiting. The ad-level move is to find the uncertainty in the customer's situation and remove it visibly.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Uncertainty-removing lines in place of speed or price claims. Example: "you will know exactly what day it ships" rather than "fast shipping."
- Progress and status shown as features. Example: a dashboard screenshot with "day 4 of 30" or a tracker.
- "What happens next" copy. Example: "after you book, you get a 12-minute video explaining the first session."
- Named people and times. Example: "Sarah will call you Tuesday between 2 and 4."
- Waiting reframed. Example: "your kit is being packed by hand; here is the person packing it."
- Transparent pricing as a selling point. Example: "the price on this page is the price you pay. No call required."
- Anxiety named in the hook. Example: "if you have ever wondered whether your accountant actually filed it..."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name the uncertainty the persona lives with. "Not knowing if the lead saw your quote is worse than losing it."
- Body: show the mechanism that removes it: a notification, a named person, a schedule, a visible step list.
- Static: a screenshot of the status screen, the calendar invite, the tracking page. Boring to the business, reassuring to the buyer.
- Video: walk through "what happens after you click" in order. It is the cheapest video a business can make and it often outperforms the promise video.
- Landing page: a "what happens next" block with three steps and times. Put the refund and cancel path in plain words next to the button.

## How it backfires
Perception levers must describe something real. "You will get a call Tuesday" is a claim, and if it is not true it is a misleading claim under both platform policy and consumer law. The lever also reverses when transparency exposes something ugly: a visible timeline that shows a six-week wait does not reassure unless the wait is explained. And removing uncertainty for a buyer who was not uncertain adds friction: a seven-step "what happens next" block on an impulse product makes it feel like work.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- At which point in your process does the customer most often email to ask what is happening?
- What do customers not know between paying and getting the result, and could you show it?
- Which of your "slow" steps could be made visible instead of faster?
