# Implicit goals (Phil Barden, Decoded, 2013)

## The idea in three sentences
Barden separates explicit goals (what the product does: cleans, saves time, books clients) from implicit goals (what the purchase means: security, autonomy, excitement, adventure, discipline, enjoyment). The strongest ads hit an implicit goal through a concrete signal, so that the viewer's fast system recognises the meaning before the slow system reads the features. The library's detection question is: what does the visual world of the ad promise beyond the product?

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- A visual world that promises a feeling the product does not literally deliver. Example: a bookkeeping app shot in a calm, empty studio; the implicit goal is control or peace.
- Colour and pace as signal. Example: fast cuts and saturated colour (excitement) versus slow pans and neutrals (security).
- Casting that signals the goal. Example: an older, calm presenter for a security goal; a restless, energetic one for adventure.
- Copy that names the meaning. Example: "run your own day" (autonomy) rather than "scheduling software."
- Product shown as a tool for an identity. Example: the discipline goal: a dawn gym, a filled-in tracker, a crossed-out list.
- Competitor sets that all pick the same implicit goal. Example: every coaching ad promising freedom; nobody promising discipline.
- A mismatch between visual world and offer. Example: a budget product with a luxury visual world; flag it, it is usually a weak ad.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: pick one implicit goal per persona from the persona file and write the first line to it. "For people who want the evenings back" is autonomy; "for people who want to stop guessing" is security.
- Body: keep the explicit goal (what it does) short and let the implicit goal set the tone of every sentence.
- Static: choose setting, light, colour and casting for the implicit goal before choosing the product shot.
- Video: pace and sound carry implicit goals more than words. Match the edit rhythm to the goal.
- Landing page: the hero image should pay off the same implicit goal as the ad; a mismatch is a drop-off point.

## How it backfires
Implicit goals must not be pushed into claims the product cannot support; "freedom" imagery on a product that requires daily effort produces refunds. Visual worlds that imply the viewer lacks the goal (anxious, undisciplined, broke) are the personal-attributes problem again. Barden's six-goal list is a model, not a law; treat it as a vocabulary for the persona file rather than a finding with a fixed number. And if every competitor in the market map promises the same implicit goal, matching it is parity; the opposite pass should test a different goal.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- Beyond what your product does, what does buying it mean to the customer: safety, freedom, excitement, discipline, enjoyment, adventure?
- Which of those does every competitor already promise?
- What does your current visual world promise, and did you choose it?
