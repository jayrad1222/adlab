# Status and identity (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
Most purchases are partly about who the buyer gets to be, and to whom; the product is the excuse, the identity is the purchase. Sutherland's version is that status-seeking is not vanity but a rational response to living among other people, and that ads which ignore it are leaving the strongest motive on the table. The interrogation asks: after buying, who does the customer get to look like, and in front of whom?

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- The buyer described, not the product. Example: "for founders who would rather be building than posting."
- An audience for the purchase named. Example: "the kind of gift that makes your mother-in-law go quiet."
- Before-and-after identity, not before-and-after result. Example: "from the friend who is always late to the one who organises the trip."
- Visible-use framing. Example: the product shown in public, on a desk others see, in a story others watch.
- In-group signals: slang, references, inside jokes. Example: a creator ad that uses the niche's own vocabulary without explaining it.
- Exclusion as status. Example: "not for everyone, and that is the point."
- Membership language. Example: "members," "the community," "people in the group."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name who the buyer gets to be. "Be the one who already has the answer in the meeting."
- Body: name the audience for the status (clients, partner, peers, the self) and the moment it becomes visible.
- Static: show the product in use in front of other people, or the artefact others will see (the certificate, the result, the post).
- Video: a customer describing how someone else reacted. Third-party reaction is the proof of status.
- Landing page: a "who this is for" block written as identity, and a visible community or cohort if you have one.

## How it backfires
Status is the mechanism most likely to breach Meta's personal attributes policy: lines that imply the viewer's current status ("stop being the broke one in the group chat") get rejected and read as insulting. Write the aspiration, not the deficit. Status claims that the product cannot deliver produce the sharpest disappointment, because the buyer feels exposed. Exclusivity also reverses in price-led categories, where "not for everyone" reads as "not for me." And identity sold too hard looks like a cult; the critic should check that the product is still visible under the identity.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- After buying, who does the customer get to look like, and in front of whom?
- Which of your customers show the product to other people, and how?
- What group does the buyer want to belong to, and does the ad use that group's language?
