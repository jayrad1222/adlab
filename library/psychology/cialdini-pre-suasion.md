# Pre-Suasion (Robert Cialdini, Pre-Suasion, 2016)

## The idea in three sentences
*Pre-Suasion* argues that what you draw attention to just before the message decides how the message is received: attention equals importance, and the privileged moment is the instant before the ask. For ads, this means the first frame sets what the viewer thinks the ad is about, and the hook is judged inside that frame. The practical rule is to choose the opener for what it makes salient, not for how it looks.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- A first frame that is not the product. Example: an image of a cluttered desk before a productivity app appears; the clutter is the pre-suasion.
- A question that primes a value. Example: "do you consider yourself adventurous?" before a travel offer.
- A single word or concept on screen before the pitch. Example: "Safe." then the insurance offer.
- A scene that sets the reference class. Example: a luxury setting before a mid-priced product so the product reads as a bargain.
- Media context used deliberately. Example: a placement next to content about the problem the product solves.
- A focus on a feeling before a fact. Example: a creator describing the anxiety of a late payment for five seconds before mentioning the invoicing tool.
- The ad's own framing line. Example: "this is not an ad for X, it is an ad for Y."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: decide what the viewer should be thinking about in second one, then choose the image and the line to make that salient. Test openers by what they prime, not by click-through alone.
- Body: keep the primed concept alive. If the opener primed "control," the body should pay off control, not speed.
- Static: the dominant visual element is the pre-suasion. If it is the product, you have primed "product"; if it is the situation, you have primed the situation.
- Video: the first 1.5 seconds before the hook line is the privileged moment. A relevant scene, sound or word there changes how the hook is read.
- Landing page: the hero image and the first line are the pre-suasion for the whole page. Match them to the ad's opener so the primed frame carries over.

## How it backfires
Priming is weak and short-lived, and the priming literature has notable replication failures; treat specific priming effects as contested and test rather than assume. A pre-suasive frame that does not match the product produces attention and then disappointment: a luxury opener on a budget offer makes the price read as cheap in the wrong sense. Media-context placement is partly out of your hands on Meta and TikTok. And priming fear or insecurity before the ask edges into the alarmist and personal-attributes territory that platform policy rejects.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What should a customer be thinking about in the second before they see your offer?
- What is the first thing on your landing page, and what does it make important?
- What are customers usually doing or feeling in the moment they see your ad?
