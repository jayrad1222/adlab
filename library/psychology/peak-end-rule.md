# The peak-end rule (Daniel Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, 1993)

## The idea in three sentences
People remember an experience by its most intense moment and its final moment, not by its average or its length. Kahneman and colleagues (1993) found that people preferred a longer cold-water trial with a slightly warmer ending over a shorter, colder one, and Redelmeier and Kahneman (1996) found the same pattern in patients' memories of medical procedures. For ads, the last three seconds of a video and the final step of a purchase are design decisions, and for the product, the best moment and the last moment decide whether the customer tells anyone.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- A strong closing beat. Example: the reveal, the punchline or the result shown in the final frame rather than a logo card.
- A designed peak inside the product. Example: "the moment your first payment lands" shown as the ad's climax.
- End-of-journey moments as creative. Example: the graduation, the last session, the unboxing of the final piece.
- Testimonials that describe the ending. Example: "the last call, when she told me I did not need her anymore."
- Post-purchase experience advertised. Example: "what you get in the welcome box."
- Deliberately short ads that end on the best line.
- Retargeting that reminds of the peak. Example: "remember the plan we built for you."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: the hook is the first peak; it buys attention. Plan the second peak and the ending before writing the middle.
- Body: cut everything after the best line. If the proof is the peak, end on the proof.
- Static: a single-frame ad is all end; make it the best moment, not the setup.
- Video: write the last three seconds first. End on the result, the face or the line, then the CTA, never on a fade.
- Landing page: the thank-you page and the confirmation email are the end of the buying experience and the start of the retention one. Design them as carefully as the hero.

## How it backfires
A peak that the product cannot deliver is the most expensive kind of overpromise, because it is the thing the customer remembers and compares against. Ending an ad on the price or the fine print makes the fine print the memory. The rule also reverses on the platform side: an ad that ends abruptly to "end on a high" can read as broken, and the CTA still needs its own beat. The original studies are small and lab-based; the effect is robust enough to design around but not strong enough to override a bad middle. And a wonderful onboarding peak followed by a dull month produces churn with a smile.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What is the single best moment a customer has with you, and when does it happen?
- What is the last thing a customer experiences in your process, and who designed it?
- What do customers say when they describe you to a friend? That is usually the peak.
