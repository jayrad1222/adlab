# SUCCESs (Chip Heath and Dan Heath, Made to Stick, 2007)

## The idea in three sentences
The Heaths' SUCCESs is a checklist for ideas that stick: simple (one core idea), unexpected (break a pattern to get attention, then open a curiosity gap to hold it), concrete (sensory, specific language), credible (proof the audience can test), emotional (make them care about a person, not a statistic) and stories (narratives people can simulate and act on). Their diagnosis of why most communication fails is the curse of knowledge: the expert cannot remember what it is like not to know. For ads, the checklist doubles as the critic's pass on any draft and as a repair kit for copy written by the business owner.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Simple: one idea per ad. Example: an ad that says one thing three ways, versus one that lists eight features (flag the eight).
- Unexpected: a pattern break in the first line. Example: "do not buy this if..." or a claim that contradicts the category.
- Concrete: nouns you can picture. Example: "the Tuesday invoice" versus "financial efficiency."
- Credible: testable proof. Example: "try it on your own numbers" or a named, checkable customer.
- Emotional: a person, not a percentage. Example: one customer's situation before the product.
- Stories: a beginning, a problem, a turn. Example: a creator's "I almost gave up" narrative.
- Curse-of-knowledge failures in the competitor set. Example: jargon in the hook, acronyms unexplained.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: one idea, stated in words a stranger would understand, with a pattern break. The two-second stranger test is the check.
- Body: replace every abstraction with a thing. Replace every statistic with a person, then put the statistic on the landing page.
- Static: a single concrete object or scene that carries the one idea.
- Video: a story with a visible turn. Open the curiosity gap in the first line and close it before the CTA.
- Landing page: credible proof the visitor can test themselves (a calculator, a sample, a demo), not only testimonials.

## How it backfires
"Unexpected" pushed too far becomes clickbait, which the platforms penalise and which trains the audience to distrust the brand. Emotional appeals that single out a person's vulnerability (debt, illness, loneliness) are policy and ethics problems. Simplicity can strip out a necessary qualification; a simple claim that is only true with conditions is a misleading claim. Stories must be real; composite or invented customer stories are deceptive under FTC guidance unless clearly labelled. And a sticky idea is not automatically a selling idea: the checklist improves recall and comprehension, and the finance gate still decides.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- If you could say only one sentence about your product to a stranger, what is it?
- Which word in your current ad would your customer have to look up?
- What proof could a sceptical prospect check themselves in five minutes?
