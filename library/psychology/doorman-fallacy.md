# The doorman fallacy (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
A hotel that replaces its doorman with an automatic door saves a salary and loses the security, the status, the welcome and the taxi-hailing that the doorman actually provided, because the job description said "opens doors." Sutherland calls this the doorman fallacy: efficiency drives that define a role too narrowly and strip out the thing that carried the meaning. For a small business the question is reversed: which inefficiency in your offer is actually the point, and does the ad show it?

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- An inefficiency sold as a feature. Example: "every plan is written by a human, not generated," with the human on screen.
- Capacity limits stated as policy, not scarcity. Example: "we take eight clients a quarter so each one gets me."
- Slow, manual or in-person steps given prominence. Example: a physio clinic advertising the 45-minute first assessment, not the treatment.
- The opposite failure, visible in the competitor set: a category where everyone has automated the same step, and nobody mentions it. Example: twelve coaching ads that all promise "AI-personalised plans."
- Named people where competitors have a chatbot. Example: "text Dana, she answers."
- Rituals of welcome. Example: a video of the onboarding call, the welcome box, the first-week check-in.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name the thing the category removed and you kept. "We still answer the phone."
- Body: explain what the "inefficient" step actually does for the customer (reassurance, status, a result the automated version cannot deliver). Say what it costs you.
- Static: a frame of the human step. The person, the handwritten note, the in-person session.
- Video: show the step in full, unhurried. Let the length of the clip carry the message that you are not rushing.
- Landing page: list the manual steps as a feature block with names and times. Resist the urge to hide them because they look expensive to deliver.

## How it backfires
Inefficiency is only a selling point if the buyer values what it produces. A slow process that produces nothing extra is just slow, and the persona will say so in the comments. "Handmade" and "personal" claims must be true; implied human involvement that is actually automated is a misleading claim under platform policy and consumer law. The fallacy also cuts the other way for the business: keeping every inefficiency because one of them matters is how margins die. The interrogation's job is to find the one that carries meaning, not to romanticise all of them.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What is the one thing you do that is inefficient on purpose, and why?
- Which step have your competitors automated that your customers secretly miss?
- If you cut your delivery cost by half tomorrow, what would customers notice first?
