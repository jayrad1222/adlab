# Anchoring and framing (Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, 1974 and 1981)

## The idea in three sentences
Anchoring is the pull of the first number seen: Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed that a random number from a wheel shifted people's estimates of unrelated quantities. Framing is the finding that logically equivalent descriptions produce different choices: beef described as 75 percent lean is rated better than beef described as 25 percent fat (Levin and Gaeth, 1988). In ads, the first number and the first description set the frame for everything after them, so they are decisions, not details.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- A high number before the price. Example: "agencies charge $5,000 for this. Ours is $497."
- A per-unit reframe. Example: "$3.20 a day" for a $97 month, or "per client" instead of "per month."
- Positive-attribute framing. Example: "93 percent of members renew" rather than "7 percent cancel."
- A crossed-out former price. Example: "$1,997 $497."
- A reference class chosen for the comparison. Example: a course compared with a university fee, not with other courses.
- Quantity anchors. Example: "most people buy three," "limit six per customer."
- Order effects. Example: the most expensive plan listed first so the middle looks reasonable.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: if the persona has a wrong reference price, state the right one first. "This usually costs a week of an agency's time."
- Body: choose the frame that is true and favourable (renewal rate, not churn rate) and keep the unit consistent.
- Static: the comparison number and the price on the same frame, with the comparison larger.
- Video: the founder states the market price before the offer, then explains the difference.
- Landing page: plan order and the "most popular" marker are anchors. Show the total alongside any per-day reframe.

## How it backfires
Anchors must be true. A crossed-out price that was never charged is deceptive pricing under FTC guidance and a misleading claim under platform policy, and comparison prices need a real reference ("agencies charge" needs a source or a hedge). Per-day reframes that hide the total are a dark pattern. Anchoring on a high number in a price-led category can read as "expensive brand" rather than "bargain." And anchors that are obviously planted ("worth $10,000, yours for $27") have been used so much in the info-product category that they now signal low quality; check the Schwartz sophistication stage.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What number does your customer have in their head before they see your price, and where did it come from?
- Which of your metrics looks better stated the other way round?
- What is the most expensive thing your customer compares you to, and is that comparison fair?
