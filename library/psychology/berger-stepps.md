# STEPPS (Jonah Berger, Contagious, 2013)

## The idea in three sentences
Berger's STEPPS is a checklist of six drivers that make ideas and products get talked about: social currency (sharing it makes me look good), triggers (something in the environment reminds people of it), emotion (high-arousal emotions travel: awe, anger, anxiety, amusement; sadness does not), public (visible use), practical value (useful things get passed on) and stories (the idea rides inside a narrative). For ads it is a critic's checklist: an ad that scores on none of the six will not be shared, and an ad that scores on two or three is a candidate for organic reach beyond its paid audience.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Social currency: insider knowledge or an unusual fact. Example: "most accountants will not tell you this."
- Triggers: a tie to a frequent cue. Example: "every time you hear the Slack ping," a Monday-morning hook.
- Emotion: an opening that produces awe, amusement or anxiety in the first two seconds. Example: a surprising before-and-after, a joke, a near-miss story.
- Public: the product visible in use by others. Example: branded packaging, a sticker, a public result.
- Practical value: a tip the viewer can use without buying. Example: "the three-line email that gets quotes answered."
- Stories: a narrative with a person, a problem and a turn, not a list of features.
- Share prompts in comments. Example: "tag someone who still does this by hand."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: lead with practical value or social currency; both earn the share and the save, which the platforms reward.
- Body: wrap the offer in a story with a named person and a turn. Keep the useful tip intact even if the viewer never buys.
- Static: a tip card that stands alone; the brand is the byline.
- Video: build the trigger in ("next time you open the invoice folder..."). End on the shareable line.
- Landing page: give the viewer a reason to show someone: a result they can post, a referral artefact, a public badge.

## How it backfires
High-arousal emotion includes anger and anxiety, and both are policy risks: anger-bait ads breach Meta's standards on divisive content, and anxiety hooks drift into the alarmist category. Social currency built on secrets ("what they don't want you to know") is now a recognised scam pattern and is treated as such by both audiences and review systems. Practical value given away can cannibalise a low-ticket product. And virality is not the goal for a small business account; a shareable ad that does not sell is a vanity metric, so the finance gate still applies.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What is the one genuinely useful thing you could tell a prospect that they could use today without buying?
- When does your customer think of you, unprompted? What is the trigger?
- What would a customer be proud to post after using you?
