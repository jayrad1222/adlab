# Jobs to be Done: the four forces (Bob Moesta and Clayton Christensen, Competing Against Luck, 2016)

## The idea in three sentences
People hire a product to make progress in a situation, and the decision to switch is governed by four forces: the push of the current situation, the pull of the new solution, the anxiety about the new, and the habit of the present. A sale happens when push plus pull exceeds anxiety plus habit. Ad Lab's persona files must fill all four, because each maps to a part of the ad: hooks name the push, bodies supply the pull and lower the anxiety, and the landing page dismantles the habit.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Push named in the hook. Example: "still doing payroll by hand at 11pm?" or "the third client this month who did not pay."
- Pull shown as a concrete new state. Example: "Friday afternoons off," a screenshot of the full calendar.
- Anxiety addressed directly. Example: "here is exactly what the first week looks like," "you do not need to learn anything new."
- Habit acknowledged. Example: "your spreadsheet works, until it does not," "keep your current tools."
- Trigger events named. Example: "just hired your first employee?" "lost a client to a cheaper quote?"
- Switching stories. Example: a customer describing the day they decided, and what tipped it.
- Ads that only supply pull. Example: feature lists with no push and no anxiety handling; flag these as half an ad.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name the push in the customer's words from the interrogation. The push is the moment, not the demographic.
- Body: one concrete pull (the new state, dated), then the anxiety answered with specifics (time to set up, what it costs to leave, who helps).
- Static: push on the left, pull on the right, with the switching step shown as small.
- Video: a switching story told in order: the push moment, the search, the worry, the first week.
- Landing page: a "what happens in week one" block (anxiety) and a "what you keep from your current setup" block (habit), both above the testimonials.

## How it backfires
Push framing that describes the viewer's distress is where Meta's personal-attributes and alarmist-content rules bite; write the situation ("the quote that is still in drafts"), not the person ("you are drowning"). Overstating the pull creates the anxiety it is meant to remove. Anxiety-lowering claims ("set up in ten minutes") are claims and must be true. And the forces are a diagnostic, not a formula: if the interrogation cannot find a real push in the customer's own words, the problem is positioning or product, and no ad structure fixes that.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- When someone decides to buy, what just happened in their life? What was the push?
- What do they worry will go wrong in the first week after switching?
- What are they currently using, including nothing, and what would they miss about it?
