# Make the invisible visible (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
Trust is built by showing the work: the restaurant with the glass-walled kitchen is trusted more than the one with a closed door, even if both kitchens are equally clean. Sutherland's argument is that most of what a business does for a customer is invisible, and that invisible effort earns no credit; the fix is to show process, people and behind-the-scenes rather than only outcomes. Buell and Norton (2011) found that people valued a travel-search result more when they could see the search happening, which they named the labour illusion.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Process shown in place of result. Example: the plan being written, the product being packed, the code being reviewed.
- The founder's hands. Example: close-ups of someone doing the work, not a logo animation.
- Screenshots of the inside. Example: the real dashboard, the real group chat, the real calendar.
- Counting the work. Example: "we checked 1,214 listings to find these 30."
- Behind-the-scenes formats in UGC. Example: a day-in-the-life of the person delivering the service.
- Progress made visible to the customer. Example: a tracker, a status email, a "we are on step 3 of 5" screen.
- Imperfection left in. Example: a messy desk, a crossed-out note, a retake.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: show the work in the first frame. "This is what happens to your file after you send it."
- Body: describe the steps between payment and result, with the people who do them.
- Static: a photo of the real process, not a stock image of a process. The messier the more credible, within brand limits.
- Video: a process clip with ambient sound; voice-over optional. Screen recordings of the actual product.
- Landing page: a "how it works" block with real screenshots, names and times, placed before the testimonials.

## How it backfires
Showing the work only builds trust if the work looks competent; a visible mess that reads as chaos has the opposite effect. Staged "behind the scenes" is detected quickly and costs more trust than it buys. Showing the inside of a product also exposes it to comparison, so a thin product should not do this. Screenshots must not include customer data, which is both a privacy problem and a platform problem. And the labour illusion has a limit: a visible process that takes a long time can make the wait feel longer if the steps look pointless.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What do you do between payment and delivery that the customer never sees?
- What would a customer find surprising if they watched you work for a day?
- Which part of your process are you proud of but have never photographed?
