# The Choice Factory (Richard Shotton, The Choice Factory, 2018)

## The idea in three sentences
Richard Shotton's *The Choice Factory* covers 25 behavioural biases chosen for commercial relevance, each with an applied experiment rather than a lab citation alone, so it tells you what to do and not only what people do. The list below is the subset the seed is confident appears in the book. Verify this list against the book's table of contents before treating it as complete.

### The biases the seed is confident about
Fundamental attribution error (context beats personality; target moments, not people); social proof and its inverse, negative social proof ("most people don't..." normalises the bad behaviour); habit (hard to break except at life transitions: new job, new baby, new city); the pain of payment; the danger of claimed data (people misreport why they buy; watch behaviour); mood and media context; price relativity and anchoring; the primacy effect; expectancy (branding changes taste); confirmation bias; overconfidence; the wisdom of crowds; the pratfall effect (admitting a flaw raises trust); the winner's curse; scarcity; the Goldilocks effect (three options, the middle sells); the power of "because" (Langer's copier study: a reason, even a weak one, raises compliance); charm pricing; the curse of knowledge; distinctiveness (the Von Restorff effect: the odd one out is remembered); and the replication caveat (not every bias replicates; prefer the robust ones).

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- An admitted flaw. Example: "it is expensive, and here is why" (pratfall).
- Three tiers with the middle highlighted (Goldilocks).
- "Because" doing the work. Example: "we ask for a deposit because it doubles show-up rates."
- A life-transition hook. Example: "just moved? just hired? just had a baby?" (habit).
- 99 or 97 endings on the price (charm pricing).
- An odd-one-out visual in a uniform feed (distinctiveness).
- "Most people" lines, either as proof or as the inverse mistake. Example: "most owners never check this" normalises not checking (negative social proof).
- Counts of others. Example: "join 3,000 clinics" (social proof, wisdom of crowds).

## Use it in ours
- Hook: a pratfall opening ("we are slower than the apps") or a transition moment ("first month in the new premises").
- Body: give a "because" after every ask. Replace "most people don't" with "more and more people do."
- Static: make it the odd one out against the market map, not against your own last ad.
- Video: a creator admitting the one thing they did not like, then why they still use it.
- Landing page: three tiers with the target in the middle; a visible reason next to every price and every form field.

## How it backfires
Shotton's own caveat is the biggest one: some biases in the popular literature have failed to replicate, and the library should prefer the robust ones (social proof, defaults, framing, loss framing used carefully) over the fragile ones. Fake pratfalls ("our only flaw is we care too much") read as the trick they are. Scarcity claims must be true under platform policy. Social proof numbers must be real and current under FTC rules. And charm pricing signals "deal," which is the wrong signal for a premium offer.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What is the one honest flaw in your offer that you could admit in the first line?
- What life transition tends to precede a purchase from you?
- Which of your asks (deposit, form, call) has no stated reason next to it?
