# Satisficing (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019; term from Herbert Simon, 1956)

## The idea in three sentences
Buyers are not trying to pick the best option; they are trying to avoid a catastrophe, and will happily accept "good enough" if it removes the risk of looking stupid. Herbert Simon named this satisficing, and Sutherland's version is the old line "nobody got fired for buying IBM." For ads this means the fear of a bad outcome has to be removed before the dream of a good one can do any work.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Safety language ahead of benefit language. Example: "trusted by 2,000 clinics" as the headline, with the actual service in the subhead.
- Brand-name reassurance. Example: logos of known customers or partners in the first frame.
- Guarantees and exits. Example: "30-day refund, cancel in two clicks" stated before the price.
- Social proof framed as safety, not popularity. Example: "join 400 agencies who switched and did not look back."
- Explicit reversal of the fear. Example: "you will not have to learn new software."
- "Nobody regrets" or "the safe choice" framing. Example: "the option your accountant would pick."
- Long, calm testimonials that mention what did not go wrong. Example: "I was worried it would be another thing I pay for and never use."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name the fear directly and take it off the table. "You will not get stuck with another course you never finish."
- Body: list the three catastrophes the persona fears (wasted money, wasted time, looking foolish) and answer each with a concrete mechanism, not an adjective.
- Static: proof that others like them made the choice and survived: a wall of faces, a count, a known logo if you have one.
- Video: a testimonial structured as "what I was afraid of, what actually happened." Ask for the fear on camera.
- Landing page: refund terms, cancellation steps and "what happens after you click" within one scroll of the button. The safest-looking option usually wins the compare.

## How it backfires
Fake social proof and inflated counts are both policy violations and quick to be caught; Meta's policy covers misleading claims and the FTC covers endorsements and testimonials. "Trusted by" with no named trusters signals the opposite. Over-reassurance can also make an offer feel low-stakes and low-value: if nothing can go wrong, nothing much happens either. For a premium or ambitious persona, satisficing copy can read as timid; those buyers want to be told they are making a bold choice, then be made safe quietly on the page.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What is the worst thing a buyer fears will happen if this goes wrong? What would make them look foolish?
- What does "doing nothing" cost them, in their own words?
- Which proof do you have that you are not using because it feels too small or too odd?
