# Price is information (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
The price is the first piece of copy most buyers read, and it tells them what kind of thing the product is before any argument lands. A higher price can raise perceived efficacy; Shiv, Carmon and Ariely (2005) found that people who paid full price for an energy drink solved more puzzles than people who paid a discounted price for the same drink. Charm pricing (ending in 9) reads as "deal," round pricing reads as "quality," and the interrogation asks what your price says about you before anyone reads your copy.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- The price position in the headline. Example: "$2,000 and worth it" versus "only $47."
- Charm endings. Example: $97, $497, $1,997 signal deal-led; $100, $500, $2,000 signal premium or simplicity.
- Reference prices invoked. Example: "less than a coffee a day," "what you paid your last agency for one month."
- Price justified by cost. Example: "we charge more because a person writes every plan."
- Price absent. Example: "book a call" with no number, which signals high-ticket and a sales process.
- Discounting frequency. Example: a competitor whose every ad in the library has a sale; the price has stopped carrying information.
- Payment framing. Example: "3 payments of $333" versus "$999," or "per day" versus "per year."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: if the price is a strength, lead with it. "Yes, it is $3,000. Here is what that buys."
- Body: tell the viewer what the price means (who it is for, what it excludes, what it signals) rather than apologising for it.
- Static: match the visual quality to the price position. A $2,000 offer in a budget-looking static is a contradiction the viewer feels.
- Video: let the founder say the number out loud and explain it. A spoken price with a reason outperforms a hidden one for many high-ticket offers; test it.
- Landing page: choose charm or round endings deliberately and keep them consistent across tiers. Show the reference class you want to be judged against.

## How it backfires
Price-as-signal only works when delivery matches: a premium price with a thin product produces refunds and reviews. Constant discounting trains the market to wait, and "was $997, now $97" claims must be truthful about the former price or they are deceptive pricing under FTC rules. Payment-plan framing has to show the total. The placebo-by-price effect is a real finding, but it is one study in one setting; treat "higher price makes it work better" as a hypothesis to test, not a rule to apply.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What does your price say about you before anyone reads your copy?
- If you had to make the product more expensive and better, what would you add?
- What are customers comparing your price to, and is that the comparison you want?
