# Availability and vividness (Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, "Availability," 1973)

## The idea in three sentences
People judge how likely or common something is by how easily examples come to mind, which Tversky and Kahneman (1973) named the availability heuristic. Vivid, recent and concrete examples come to mind more easily than statistics, so a single detailed story outweighs a table of averages. For ads, one specific customer with a name, a place and a number is more persuasive than "thousands of customers," and the same mechanism is why fear-based categories over-index on the dramatic case.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- One named customer instead of a count. Example: "Priya, a dentist in Leeds, added 11 patients in March."
- Sensory detail. Example: the exact time, the sound of the notification, the colour of the folder.
- A recent-event hook. Example: "after last week's outage..." or "since the new rules in April."
- The dramatic case used as the typical case. Example: a single extreme result presented as what to expect.
- Visual specificity in frames. Example: a real desk, a real screen, not a stock image of "success."
- Repetition across the account. Example: the same story told in five ads so it becomes the thing people recall.
- Easy-to-picture outcomes. Example: "Friday afternoons off" rather than "improved work-life balance."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: one person, one place, one number. The hook taxonomy calls this the specific-story opener.
- Body: tell the story in order with concrete nouns. Save the aggregate numbers for the landing page.
- Static: a photo of the actual customer or the actual artefact, with permission, over a stock composite.
- Video: a customer telling their own story with details the scriptwriter would not have invented.
- Landing page: a few vivid cases above the aggregate proof, each with name, context and result, plus the "results vary" disclosure where needed.

## How it backfires
Vividness makes the atypical feel typical, which is exactly what FTC endorsement rules police: a dramatic result shown as expected needs a clear and conspicuous disclosure of what buyers generally achieve, and in some categories (health, income) Meta restricts such claims regardless. Vivid fear examples breach the alarmist-content rules. Stories must be real and consented. And the heuristic reverses when the vivid example is too far from the persona: a story about a London agency makes a sole trader in Perth feel the product is not for them.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- Which single customer story do you tell most often when someone asks what you do?
- What is the most ordinary good result you get, described with a name, a place and a number?
- What recent event in your customers' world makes the problem easy to picture right now?
