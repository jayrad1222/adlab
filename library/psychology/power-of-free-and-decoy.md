# The power of free and the decoy effect (Dan Ariely, Predictably Irrational, 2008; Huber, Payne and Puto, 1982)

## The idea in three sentences
Zero is a category, not a discount: Shampanier, Mazar and Ariely (2007) found that when a premium chocolate was cut from 15 cents to 14 and a cheap one from 1 cent to free, demand swung sharply to the free one even though the price gap was unchanged. The decoy effect, first described by Huber, Payne and Puto (1982) as asymmetric dominance, is that adding a third option that is clearly worse than the target makes the target look better; Ariely's example is the magazine subscription where a print-only option at the same price as print-plus-web pushed buyers to the bundle. In ads, free elements and three-option offers are among the most visible mechanisms in any market map.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- "Free" as the headline. Example: "free shipping," "free first session," "free audit," with the paid offer behind it.
- A free element valued higher than an equivalent discount. Example: "free $30 bonus" rather than "$30 off."
- Three tiers where one is obviously a worse deal. Example: a middle plan that costs almost as much as the top plan but has far less.
- A "most popular" marker on the tier the business wants sold.
- Free conditions buried. Example: "free" with an asterisk and a required subscription.
- Zero-risk framing. Example: "nothing to pay today," "$0 to start."
- Free-plus-shipping offers. Example: a physical product free with a shipping charge that covers the cost.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: if something is genuinely free, say the word early and say the condition in the same breath. "Free audit. No call required."
- Body: present the free element as the first step of the ritual, not as bait.
- Static: three tiers with the target in the middle, with the decoy designed honestly (fewer features at a slightly lower price) rather than deceptively.
- Video: a creator showing what they got for free and what it led them to buy, in that order.
- Landing page: a free entry point with no card, and a plan table where the comparison makes the target obvious. Show the total for every tier.

## How it backfires
"Free" is regulated: the FTC's guidance on the use of the word free requires that conditions be stated clearly and that the price of the paired item not be inflated to cover the "free" one. Meta rejects ads where "free" leads to a trap. Free offers attract low-intent traffic; the finance decision rules should check cost per qualified lead, not cost per lead, before scaling a free hook. Decoys that are too obvious insult the buyer and are now widely recognised in SaaS pricing pages. And "free" reverses in premium categories: a free first session at a high-ticket clinic signals desperation, where a paid-but-refundable one signals confidence.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What could you give away free that costs you little and proves the product?
- If you have three plans, which one do you want sold, and does the table make that obvious?
- What does "free" signal about you in your category: generosity or desperation?
