# Context changes value (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
The same product is worth different amounts in different contexts, so changing the context is often cheaper than changing the product. Sutherland's examples: rather than spending billions making Eurostar faster, make the journey more pleasant; Uber's map did not shorten the wait, it changed what waiting felt like. In an ad, the context is set in the first two seconds, which makes "where and when the product is imagined" one of the cheapest levers available.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- The product shown somewhere the category never shows it. Example: meal-prep containers shot at a football pitch, not a kitchen.
- A time-of-day or moment frame. Example: "the 11pm invoice run" for bookkeeping software.
- A comparison to a different reference class. Example: a $97 course priced against "one dinner out," not against other courses.
- Occasion-led copy. Example: "for the week before your wedding," "for your first winter as a landlord."
- Use with a different companion. Example: a kids' learning app shown being used by a grandparent.
- A category swap in the headline. Example: a gym that calls itself "a third place," not a gym.
- Situational hooks in UGC. Example: a creator opening with "I am in the car park before a client meeting and..."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: move the product into a new moment. Name the situation, not the product: "the Sunday night before the school week."
- Body: describe what the product does in that moment, and let the moment set the reference price.
- Static: change the background before you change anything else. Test the same product in three settings.
- Video: open in the situation (the car, the clinic waiting room, the kitchen at 6am) and let the product enter it.
- Landing page: match the page's context to the ad's context. If the ad sold a moment, the page should open on the same moment, not on a feature list.

## How it backfires
A context that the persona does not recognise reads as a stock photo. A context chosen for novelty with no mechanism (why would value be higher here?) is just a different photo. Some contexts trigger policy problems: Meta restricts imagery that implies health conditions, financial distress or other personal attributes, so "shown in a hospital bed" or "shown at a debt collector's desk" needs care. And context cannot rescue a product that is wrong for the moment; a heavy software onboarding sold as "set up on the train" will produce refund requests.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- When someone decides to buy, what just happened in their life?
- Where is your product never shown, and would it be worth more there?
- What category are you letting the customer judge you in, and is there a better one?
