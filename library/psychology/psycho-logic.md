# Psycho-logic (Rory Sutherland, Alchemy, 2019)

## The idea in three sentences
People decide through a parallel logic that handles meaning, risk, status and context, and then post-rationalize the choice in terms of features and price. Sutherland calls this psycho-logic, and argues it is not a bug but a reasonable way to act with incomplete information. The ad's job is to satisfy psycho-logic first; the rational case only gets heard once the viewer already feels the choice is safe and fitting.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Long-running ads with weak feature claims. Example: an ad that says only "the one your friends keep mentioning" and has been live for six months in the ad library.
- Copy that answers "what does this say about me" before "what does this do." Example: "for coaches who are tired of looking like everyone else on Instagram."
- Reassurance before benefit. Example: the first line is "cancel any time, no questions," the benefit comes second.
- A reason given that is not really a reason. Example: "we charge more because we are slower." The line works on meaning, not economics.
- Visual signals of care rather than performance. Example: hand-labelled jars, a workshop, a person checking the product.
- A story in place of a comparison table. Example: a 40-second testimonial that never mentions a feature.

## Use it in ours
- Hook: open on the feeling the viewer needs confirmed (safety, belonging, not being foolish), not on the specification.
- Body: give one psycho-logical reason (who this is for, what could go wrong and why it will not) and one rational reason (what it costs, what you get). Keep them in that order.
- Static: one image that answers "is this for people like me." Faces, settings and objects that match the persona's world.
- Video: the first three seconds settle meaning (who, where, what kind of brand). Features can wait until the viewer has decided they belong.
- Landing page: put the post-rationalization material (comparison, specs, FAQ) below the fold. Above the fold, settle status, risk and context.

## How it backfires
Treating psycho-logic as permission to say anything. The mechanism explains why weak claims can work; it does not make false claims safe, and Meta's and the FTC's rules judge the literal claim, not the feeling. Identity-based lines that imply the viewer has a personal characteristic (health condition, financial state, age) breach Meta's personal attributes policy. Psycho-logic also reverses when the "meaning" offered conflicts with the persona's self-image: a luxury frame on a budget audience reads as mockery.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What does a customer need to feel is true before your rational argument can land?
- When a customer explains the purchase to a partner or boss, what do they say, and is that the real reason?
- Which of your claims would survive if you deleted every number from the page?
