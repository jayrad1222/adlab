# Defaults and status quo bias (Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, Nudge, 2008; Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988)

## The idea in three sentences
People disproportionately stick with the current state and with whatever option is preselected: Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988) named this status quo bias, and Johnson and Goldstein (2003) showed that countries with opt-out organ donation had far higher enrolment than opt-in countries. Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge* turned this into choice architecture: whoever sets the default sets the outcome. For ads, the status quo is the real competitor, and on the page the default is the real salesperson.

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- The status quo named as the enemy. Example: "your spreadsheet is fine until it is not."
- Switching cost removed. Example: "we migrate your data for you," "cancel the old one, we will handle it."
- A preselected plan or add-on visible in a landing-page screenshot. Example: the annual plan highlighted and prechecked.
- "Do nothing" addressed directly. Example: "if you do nothing, next quarter looks like this one."
- A trial that converts by default. Example: "free for 14 days, then $29 unless you cancel."
- Habit anchors. Example: "same login, same workflow, one new button."
- Switching made to look normal. Example: "most of our members came from X."

## Use it in ours
- Hook: name the current state honestly, without mocking it. "You are still doing this in a group chat."
- Body: list the switching steps and who does them. The anxiety about switching is a status-quo force; shrink it with specifics.
- Static: before and after of the workflow (not of a person), with the switching step shown as small.
- Video: a customer walking through the day they switched. "It took the afternoon."
- Landing page: choose the default plan deliberately and make it the one most buyers should pick. Preselect nothing the buyer would resent discovering.

## How it backfires
Defaults that trap are dark patterns: prechecked add-ons, trials that bill without clear notice, and cancellation paths hidden behind calls have drawn FTC enforcement and are banned or restricted by app stores and platforms. The persuasion gain is small compared with the refund and review cost. Status-quo copy that ridicules the viewer's current method loses them. And the bias cuts against you as well: a new brand asking people to leave a familiar one needs more reassurance than a familiar brand asking for a renewal, which is why the switching-cost section of the body usually matters more than the benefit section.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation
- What does the customer do instead today, including nothing, and what would it take to leave it?
- What is preselected on your checkout, and would the customer be glad or annoyed to notice it?
- How long does switching actually take, and could you say that number in the ad?
