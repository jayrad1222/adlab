# Halbert, Sugarman, Bencivenga and Collier (Gary Halbert, the Boron Letters, 1984; Joe Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*, 2006; Gary Bencivenga, the Bencivenga Bullets; Robert Collier, *The Robert Collier Letter Book*, 1931)

## The idea in three sentences

The market matters more than the copy: find the starving crowd, then make the ad look like something they would open anyway. Once opened, every sentence has one job, to get the next sentence read, and the lead keeps planting small open loops so the reader slides to the offer. The offer itself persuades when it stacks an urgent problem, a unique promise, unquestionable proof and an easy proposition, and it lands only if it enters the conversation already going on in the reader's mind.

Halbert gave us the starving crowd and A-pile versus B-pile mail (the personal-looking envelope gets opened; today the native-looking ad gets watched). Sugarman gave us the slippery slide and seeds of curiosity ("but there's more"). Bencivenga gave us the persuasion equation. Collier gave us the entry point: start where the reader already is.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- **Starving crowd targeting.** The ad names a hungry situation, not a demographic: "the week before your first tenant moves in," not "landlords." Look for a moment, a deadline or a pain the viewer is already living.
- **A-pile disguise.** The creative looks like a text message, a note, a selfie video or a screenshot rather than an ad. Tag it as native format in the per-ad rubric and check whether the content keeps the promise the disguise makes.
- **Slippery slide construction.** Short first sentence. Each line ends on an unresolved thought. The transcript reads as one pull rather than a list of points. Count how many sentences pass before the first full stop that could end the ad.
- **Seeds of curiosity.** Phrases that promise a later payoff: "and that was before I found the second leak." Note where each seed is paid off, or whether it never is.
- **The persuasion equation in order.** Problem stated as urgent, promise stated as unique, proof shown rather than claimed, proposition made easy (trial, small first step, guarantee). An ad that has three of the four usually fails on proof.
- **Collier's entry.** The opening line is something the reader was already thinking that morning, often in their own words from reviews or comments.

## Use it in ours

- **Hook:** write the sentence the persona said in a review or a community thread, then cut it to its first clause. That is Collier's entry and Halbert's A-pile in one move.
- **Body:** draft as a slide. One idea per line. End lines on a forward lean. Plant one seed of curiosity in the first third and pay it off before the CTA. Then check Bencivenga's four: urgent, unique, proof, easy. Fix the weakest one before polishing anything else.
- **Static:** a screenshot, note or typed-letter format is the A-pile envelope. The headline is the first line of the conversation in the reader's head. One proof element visible without zooming.
- **Video:** the first line is spoken, not titled. The cut rhythm is the slide; no cut should resolve the previous thought completely until the proof section. A founder-to-camera that opens with the viewer's own complaint is the format this canon was built for.
- **Landing page:** the page continues the slide. Headline repeats the hook's promise, first paragraph restates the urgent problem in the reader's language, proof block before price, the proposition made small (the first week, the first call, the trial).

## How it backfires

- A-pile disguise without substance is a bait and switch. The platform's ranking and the viewer both punish it, and the FTC rules on deceptive formats apply to fake DMs and fake screenshots; see `../platform/ftc-endorsements.md`.
- Seeds of curiosity that never pay off train the audience to skip the member's ads.
- "Unquestionable proof" is the member's job to supply, not the copywriter's to imply. A proof-shaped sentence with no proof behind it fails the evidence integrity check.
- "Urgent problem" can slide into manufactured urgency. Urgency must be true (a date, a capacity limit the member can show).
- The slippery slide written badly becomes breathless. Short sentences are a tool, not a style.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- Who is starving for this, and what moment are they in when they feel it most?
- What does the persona say in their own words the morning the problem bites? Is that line the first line of any ad?
- Which of Bencivenga's four is weakest in the current offer: urgent, unique, proof or easy?
- What would the ad look like if it were not allowed to look like an ad?
- What is the smallest true first step a buyer can take, and is it the proposition?
