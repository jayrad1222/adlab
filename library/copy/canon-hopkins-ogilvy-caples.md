# Hopkins, Ogilvy and Caples (Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising*, 1923; David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising*, 1983; John Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*, 1932)

## The idea in three sentences

An ad is a salesperson in print, so judge it by what it sells, not by whether anyone admires it. Specifics beat generalities, a reason-why beats a claim, and a headline that promises a concrete self-interest beats a clever one. Research first, test everything, and write only what you would be happy for your family to read.

Hopkins supplied the discipline (specifics, reason-why, testing). Ogilvy supplied the method (research before writing, the big idea, the headline carrying most of the value, long copy when it is interesting). Caples supplied the headline ranking: self-interest first, news second, curiosity third, in that order of reliability, plus the identification headline ("they laughed when I sat down at the piano") where the reader sees themselves in the story.

## Spot it in a competitor ad

- **A specific where a generality would do.** "Drained in 40 minutes" instead of "fast service." The number is the cue. Note whether the number is verifiable or decorative.
- **A reason-why clause.** Copy that says "because" and then explains a mechanism or a policy ("because we carry the part on the truck"). Tag the mechanism in the per-ad rubric.
- **The headline doing the selling.** The first line of the primary text or the first on-screen card carries the promise; the body only supports it. If the headline is a brand name or a pun, the ad is not working in this tradition.
- **Self-interest or news in the headline.** "Now" and "new" are Caples' news cues; "you" plus a payoff is the self-interest cue. Curiosity-only headlines (a tease with no payoff) are the weakest form in Caples' ranking.
- **Long copy that stays interesting.** A primary text that runs past the "see more" fold, with proof, a story or a demonstration, is Ogilvy's long-copy move. Check whether it is long because it has something to say or long because it is padded.
- **An identification lead.** A story where the reader is the hero and the product is the turn in the plot.

## Use it in ours

- **Hook:** lead with the most specific true benefit the member can state. Replace every adjective with a number, a time, a named part or a named outcome. Rank candidate hooks by Caples' order: self-interest, then news, then curiosity.
- **Body:** add the reason-why under every claim. If the member cannot say why the claim is true, the claim does not ship. Write long when the proof is interesting; cut when it is not.
- **Static:** one headline, one proof line, one reason-why. Hopkins' specificity test: could the number be checked by a customer? If not, remove it.
- **Video:** the first three seconds carry the headline. The demonstration is Hopkins' reason-why made visible. Let the camera prove what the copy claims.
- **Landing page:** headline matches the ad's promise word for word (Ogilvy's research-before-writing applies to the page too: use the persona's language from reviews). Long-form page when the sophistication stage demands mechanism and proof; short when the audience is most aware.

## How it backfires

- Specificity invites fabrication. A made-up number is worse than a vague claim, because it is falsifiable and will be falsified. Every number needs a source the member can show.
- Reason-why copy can become a feature list. The reason must connect to the reader's payoff, not to the member's pride.
- "Long copy works" is not "long copy always works." On a phone, long primary text that opens weak never gets expanded.
- Caples' headline ranking was tested on print and mail. Treat the order as a strong prior, not a law; the readout decides.
- Ogilvy's taste rule (nothing you would hide from your family) is a floor on truth and tone. It does not license polish for its own sake; see `../strategy/hott-ugly-ads.md`.

## Questions it adds to the interrogation

- Which claim on your site could a competitor make word for word? What is the specific that only you can say?
- For every claim: why is it true? Can a customer check it?
- What is the single most interesting true thing about how you work, and is it anywhere in your copy?
- If the headline were the only thing a stranger read, would they know the payoff of reading on?
- Which numbers in your current ads are decorative, and which are verifiable?
