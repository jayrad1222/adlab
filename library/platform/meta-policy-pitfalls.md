# Meta policy pitfalls

This file lists the rejections that catch small advertisers most often on Facebook and Instagram, and shows how to keep the idea while fixing the wording. The critic agent reads it before signing off any hook, and the live Meta Advertising Standards page always wins over anything written here.

## The rule above all rules

Meta's policies change several times a year, and enforcement is automated first, so wording that passed last month can fail today. Treat every entry below as a pattern to watch for, not a quotation of the policy. When the critic flags a line, read the live page, then rewrite.

## Implied personal attributes in the second person

Meta rejects copy that asserts or implies something about the viewer: their health, weight, finances, race, religion, sexual orientation, criminal record, or mental state. The trigger is usually the word "you" plus a condition.

How it shows up in a hook: "Are you depressed?" "Struggling with your weight?" "Drowning in debt?" "Still broke at 35?"

Rewrite that keeps the idea: talk about the condition or the group, not the viewer. "Depression makes mornings the hardest part of the day." "Most people who try to lose weight after 40 hit the same wall." "The 11pm invoice pile is a problem for two-person shops." The callout hook still works when it names a role or situation ("if you run a cleaning company") rather than a personal attribute.

## Before-and-after in health and body

Side-by-side body shots, weight numbers on a scale, skin close-ups, and "day 1 versus day 30" physique photos get rejected in health, fitness, and beauty categories. The same framing can also be rejected as implying unrealistic results.

How it shows up: a split frame, a swipe-to-reveal carousel, or a hook like "I lost 30 pounds in 30 days, here is the proof."

Rewrite: show the process, not the transformation. A day in the routine, the method, the mistake the person made first, the thing they do differently now. If the member has a real result with consent and context, the landing page or a testimonial with the person's own words carries it better than a split frame in the ad.

## Misleading income or results claims

Meta rejects ads that promise or strongly imply earnings, returns, or results that are not typical, and it applies this hard to "make money," coaching, trading, and business opportunity ads.

How it shows up: "Make $10k a month from your phone." "My students average $5k in their first 30 days." "Guaranteed clients in 14 days or your money back."

Rewrite: describe what the thing is and what the person does inside it. "A weekly build session for people selling services online." "What I would do in the first 30 days if I were starting again." A specific number can stay when it is observed and labeled as one person's result with context, but it cannot be the promise. See the truth rules file for the earnings guarantee ban, which is stricter than Meta's.

## Sensational content

Shock imagery, fear tactics, violent or graphic frames, and exaggerated negative outcomes are rejected as sensational, especially in health and finance.

How it shows up: "This mistake will bankrupt you." A hook built on disgust, a bleeding wound, a crashed car, or a crying child.

Rewrite: keep the stakes, lose the shock. "The one line in most contracts that costs people the most." Negative or warning hooks are allowed when the warning is specific and the frame is calm.

## AI-generated content disclosure

Meta requires disclosure on some ads that use AI-generated or digitally altered media, with the strictest rules on social issue, electoral, and political ads. Rules for commercial ads are evolving. Treat a photorealistic AI person, an AI voice reading as if it were the founder, or an altered real event as a disclosure case until the live page says otherwise.

How it shows up: an AI avatar presenting as a real customer; an AI voiceover over the founder's face; a generated "screenshot" of results.

Rewrite: use AI for b-roll, illustration, and clearly stylized frames. Never use it to fabricate a person, a testimonial, or a result. Where disclosure is required, add it in the ad settings and do not rely on fine print in the caption.

## Branded content rules

When a creator posts an ad for a brand from the creator's own account, Meta requires the branded content tool and the paid partnership label. Boosting a creator post without it can be rejected or taken down after approval.

How it shows up: a UGC clip posted from the creator's handle with "partner" in the caption and nothing else.

Rewrite: tag the brand as a paid partner, or run the clip from the brand's own account as a regular ad with usage rights in writing. The FTC file covers the disclosure side of the same clip.
