# FTC endorsements and disclosures

This file explains what the United States FTC Endorsement Guides require of testimonials, creator content, and founder claims, and what Ad Lab enforces on top of them. It applies to every piece of proof in the bank, whether it comes from a customer, a paid creator, or the founder on camera.

## The three tests

Every endorsement in an ad has to pass three tests. It must be real. It must be typical, or the ad must say what the typical result is. And any material connection between the endorser and the business must be disclosed. Fail one and the ad is deceptive under the Guides, whatever the platform policy says.

Other countries have their own versions with the same principle: the viewer must be able to tell a paid opinion from an unpaid one, and a cherry-picked result from a normal one.

## Real endorsements

An endorsement must reflect the honest opinion and actual experience of the person giving it. A scripted line read by an actor is not a testimonial. A founder reading a review out loud is fine when the review is real and attributed. A composite "customer" assembled from several people is not real. An AI-generated person saying anything about the product is not an endorsement at all, and presenting it as one is the problem.

The endorser must have used the product, so the brief gives the creator time with it before filming.

## Typical results, or say what is typical

If an ad shows a result, viewers will assume they can expect something similar. The Guides require that the advertiser either have evidence that the result is typical or clearly say what the typical result is. A small-print "results not typical" line does not do that job any more. The disclosure has to be clear, close to the claim, and specific about what most people get.

This hits the creator and course category hardest. "She made $8,000 in her first month" needs a statement of what members usually make, and most businesses cannot truthfully make one. The safer move is to not lead with the number and to describe the thing the person did instead.

## Why "results not typical" does not shield a fabricated result

The disclaimer exists to put context on a real but unusual result. It assumes the result happened. If the result is invented, exaggerated, or assembled from a demo that was never deployed, the disclaimer makes it worse: it shows the advertiser knew the claim needed qualifying and ran it anyway. No disclaimer fixes a false claim. Ad Lab's evidence rules say the same thing: a result goes in the bank only when it is observed or owner-provided with a named source.

## Material connections

A material connection is anything that might affect how a viewer weighs the endorsement: payment, free product, a discount, an affiliate commission, an employment or family relationship, or a chance to win something. It has to be disclosed in the content itself, in a way the viewer sees before deciding. A hashtag buried after twenty others does not count. "Paid partnership" at the top, or a spoken "they sent me this" in the first seconds, does.

The brand is responsible, not just the creator. If a brand pays for a clip and the clip omits the disclosure, both are on the hook.

## How this applies to UGC

Paid UGC is an endorsement if the creator appears to be speaking from experience. The brief must include the disclosure plan and the creator must have used the product. Keep the usage rights and the disclosure in the same written agreement. When the brand runs the clip from its own account as an ad, the paid relationship is implied by the placement, but any claim in the clip still has to be true and typical, or qualified.

## How this applies to creators

A creator posting from their own account must use the platform's paid partnership label and say plainly that it is paid. Affiliate links count as a material connection. A creator who is also a customer still has to disclose the payment.

## How this applies to founder-to-camera

A founder talking about their own product is obviously connected to it, so the connection does not need a separate disclosure. The other two tests still apply in full. A founder claiming "our members get clients in two weeks" is making a results claim and needs the same substantiation as a customer saying it. A founder reading a scripted "customer story" in the first person is misrepresenting who is speaking.

## What the critic checks

For every proof element: who is speaking, did they use the product, is any payment or relationship disclosed in the content, is the result real and sourced, and is the typical outcome stated or the specific number removed. The check goes in the bank entry under proof type and evidence status.
